BRIdex: Building the system around the library

Bank Rakyat Indonesia · Product Design Lead · Six UI designers

BRIdex combined a shared design foundation with clearer ownership, contribution, migration, and adoption practices.

From component library to operating model

How a familiar design-system problem became a question of capacity, ownership, migration, and trust.

01. A shared organization, but not a shared system

BRI had five main product squads. Around them were other products, ad-hoc dashboards, and design contributors outside our department. On paper, the design organization was centralized. In practice, teams had developed their own ways of working.

Common components existed in several versions. Documentation was incomplete. Designers inheriting a product could not always trust the components they found, so rebuilding often felt safer than reusing. BRImo and Ceria had also developed separate design languages, even though both belonged to BRI.

A more robust library seemed like the obvious answer. Before committing to that answer, I ran a workshop with product designers across streams and products.

The workshop exposed three deeper tensions

The discussion shifted the problem from component quality to the conditions surrounding the work.

What sat behind the visible inconsistency

  1. CapacityDesigners created local components to meet product deadlines, but rarely had enough time to maintain them afterward.
  2. TrustComponents and documentation were inconsistent, while implementation did not always match Figma.
  3. OwnershipTeams were unsure when to contribute, who should decide, and whether system work should take priority over immediate delivery.

The component problems were real, but they were symptoms of how the work was organized. We still needed to improve the library; a better library alone would not fix the conditions that had allowed the old one to decay.

How could we build a shared foundation that teams could use and maintain without depending on volunteer effort?

02. Before building, I had to create capacity

BRIdex had started as voluntary work. Designers contributed when product delivery allowed it, which meant progress was slow and inconsistent.

I proposed BRIdex as a departmental OKR. Rather than framing it as design cleanup, I connected it to repeated design and development effort, delivery time, onboarding, and reuse. The goal was to make the work legible as an organizational investment, not a side project for designers.

  1. OKRDepartmental priority. BRIdex moved from spare-time contribution into formally protected work.
  2. 6 × 50%Reassigned capacity. Six existing UI designers each received a 50% allocation to BRIdex.
  3. 4 + 2Two complementary focuses. Four designers worked on foundations and components; two worked on documentation, releases, migration, and adoption.

This was reassigned capacity, not new headcount. It changed BRIdex from work people attempted in their spare time into work with named ownership and protected priority.

The support had limits. I also asked for a dedicated engineer but could not secure one. BRIdex had design capacity, while engineering participation still depended on the priorities of each product team.

Looking back, the work had two layers

The system layer covered what teams could use. The operating layer covered how people would build, maintain, and adopt it. One without the other would repeat the old problem: either a library nobody maintained or a mandate built around something teams did not trust.

Designing ownership around the system

I divided the work across three groups.

  1. 01Core Component Team. Four UI designers worked on the shared foundation, reusable components, documentation, and design quality.
  2. 02Experience Team. Two UI designers focused on release communication, documentation, migration support, and adoption. I led this team and remained hands-on.
  3. 03Embedded maintainers. Product designers maintained local libraries, kept them connected to the shared foundation, and brought product needs back to BRIdex.

This distributed responsibility beyond the central team. It did not, however, guarantee that maintainers had enough capacity when their product roadmaps became busy.

03. A shared core with room for difference

BRIdex used a shared core connected to local product libraries. Foundational decisions and common components could remain shared. Product teams could keep patterns that only made sense for their own users or requirements.

This avoided forcing every product into one rigid library without returning to fully disconnected systems. We were also researching the token architecture, but I only describe that work as implemented where release evidence supports it.

Letting product needs shape the core

The contribution workflow was designed to connect local product work back to BRIdex. Instead of allowing every product need to become a permanent local component, the team could review whether it belonged in the shared core.

  1. 01Product need
  2. 02Backlog and initial concept
  3. 03Joint review and iteration
  4. 04Core release and documentation

The same process also protected products from having every local requirement forced into a universal pattern. The workflow is visible in the governance artifacts, but I still need one completed component example before claiming that the loop worked consistently in practice.

04. BRImo as the execution layer

BRIdex explains the organization and the shared system. BRImo is where those ideas met a real product, a legacy library, and an engineering team responsible for delivering the revamp.

BRImo already had a local design system, so migration was not a clean start. The design team began using BRIdex foundations while maintaining a local BRImo library for product-specific needs. From a design perspective, this was the federated model working as intended.

Engineering saw a different problem. Adopting new BRIdex components could mean rebuilding implementations that already worked. Their resistance was not simply reluctance to follow the design system; it reflected real migration cost and delivery risk.

The exact component, compromise, and production result still need to be documented. Until then, BRImo should be presented as the execution layer of the case—not as a complete migration success.

05. What changed, and where the model fell short

  1. PriorityBRIdex moved from voluntary work into a departmental priority.
  2. CapacitySix designers received named responsibility and allocated time.
  3. SystemThe team released a shared foundation with clearer roles, documentation, and a contribution workflow.
  4. LimitAdoption remained partial, and many maintainers continued using legacy local components.

The OKR protected work inside the central design team, but it did not remove migration cost from product teams. Without dedicated engineering ownership, implementation still had to be negotiated product by product.

We defined measures around design time, development effort, onboarding, adoption, and prototype speed. We did not establish a reliable baseline or collect those measures consistently. They remain evidence of how we intended to evaluate the work, not proof of business impact.

What I carried forward

BRIdex changed my understanding of design-system work. The quality of the library mattered, but it was not the hardest part. The harder questions were who had time to maintain it, who absorbed the cost of migration, and what happened when shared standards competed with a product roadmap.

Securing the OKR helped us build the foundation. It did not solve adoption on its own. If I approached the work again, I would establish engineering ownership and a migration strategy earlier, alongside the component architecture—not after the foundation was ready.
